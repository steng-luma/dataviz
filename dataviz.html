The ocean around these islands is better observed than the world's ocean on average. The air above them is barely observed at all. This is a story about which instruments exist, which ones are switched on, and what happened the last time it mattered.

01 — Where the long record came from

The long record was never about these islands

Ships logged sea temperature on their way to somewhere else. Satellites read the surface from orbit. Both cover the Pacific because the Pacific is in the way — neither was built to watch these islands. A line drawn back to 1850 is not the same thing as a country measuring its own weather, and the two come apart badly once we look at what is on the ground.

02 — The signal that hurts

Rainfall is the signal that hurts, and it swings

Temperature is the thing the long record measures well, and it is the one every summary of the Pacific reports. Rainfall is the thing people plan around, and it behaves differently. It does not climb — it lurches, and it lurches far harder than the trend ever moves.

The temperature trend was only measurable because the same places were measured the same way for decades. Rainfall asks for more than that: it moves faster and over shorter distances, so seeing it takes more instruments in more places — and it is the thing the region has fewest instruments for.

02 · continued

The swings are not random, which is what makes them forecastable in principle. In an El Niño, warm water slides east and the rain follows it — so one half of the Pacific is wet while the other is dry.

The South Pacific Convergence Zone shifts north-east toward the equator and loses its diagonal shape, so the convection migrates. Islands near the equator gain rain; islands to the south-west lose it. Which side a country falls on is set here from published climatology before looking at the rainfall data — so the split can be tested rather than assumed.

So the mechanism is known and the pattern holds. But a farmer does not plant against a pattern for half an ocean. What has to be forecast is how much rain falls in one district, and when it stops — and that can only be answered from the record of that district.

03 — The last time it mattered

Papua New Guinea, 2015

The rain began falling away in April 2015. Between mid-July and mid-August, at the one high-altitude research station recording it, temperatures fell to minus two degrees before dawn. The frosts destroyed sweet potato — the staple — in gardens above 2,200 metres, where a replanted crop takes eight to twelve months to mature.

An El Niño declaration is a statement about the ocean. A rainfall forecast for a district is built from what rainfall at that place has done before — so it can only ever be as local as the record behind it.

The ocean was watched. By early April 2015, forecasters including the Australian Bureau of Meteorology were reporting a severe El Niño moving west. What no one could say was how much rain would fall in the Kandep basin, or when it would stop.

The highlands are where most of Papua New Guinea's people live, between roughly 1,500 and 2,800 metres. They are also where almost none of the measurement is.

04 — The framework

Rainfall takes three layers

Rainfall is not one measurement. It takes three, and they do different jobs. Scroll: one of them works, one is thin and misplaced, and one has effectively gone.

The upper air

It steers the rain

Radiosondes rise twice a day and read temperature, humidity and wind through the whole depth of the atmosphere. That is what tells a forecaster where the convection is going over the next few days — the difference between knowing an El Niño is running and knowing which valley gets the rain.

It fuels the rain

05 — Why

Existing is not reporting, and who pays decides which

A station in the register is not a station sending data. WMO monitors both, and the gap between them is where the network quietly stopped. Under GBON a station counts as compliant when it delivered 80% of required reports on at least 80% of days in the month.

Papua New Guinea is registered for upper-air observation and received — of — expected soundings in July 2026. Solomon Islands and Tuvalu are also registered, and also sent nothing. The obligation is on the books. The balloons are not going up.

The two layers also come apart inside the same country — and that is the tell.

This is also what makes the gap easy to miss. Every silent station is still counted — in the register, in the network totals, and in any assessment built on how many stations a country has. On paper the region is better observed than it was thirty years ago.

06 — Close

The gap is administrative, not natural

The same logic decides what is watched. Tide gauges and rain gauges belong to different programmes, on different funding cycles, answering to different agencies.

Everything that would show the rain coming has been switched off, one station at a time — and much of what remains is registered, counted, and silent.

You cannot prepare for what you cannot watch arriving. The next El Niño will be forecast from the ocean, accurately, months ahead. Nobody will be able to say what it means for a valley in Enga.
